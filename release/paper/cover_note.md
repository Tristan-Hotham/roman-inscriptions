# Cover note: Where Romans Wrote

## Suitable journals (no fee to publish; limits checked in 2026)

1. **Digital Scholarship in the Humanities** (Oxford University Press). Full papers should not normally exceed 9,000 words, excluding notes and references. No charge to publish under the standard licence; open access is optional and paid. The paper's text before the references is about 8,300 words including tables, captions and the appendix, so it fits. The journal suits the paper's method (reproducible extraction rules over a whole digital corpus).
2. **Journal of Roman Studies** (Cambridge University Press, for the Society for the Promotion of Roman Studies). Research papers of up to 15,000 words including notes. No publication fee is stated; it is a subscription journal with optional paid open access. It published Meyer (1990), Woolf (1996), Saller and Shaw (1984) and Scheidel (2001), the main interlocutors of this paper. It fits as it stands, though the editors may want a fuller discussion of the epigraphic literature and less statistical apparatus in the main text.
3. **Digital Humanities Quarterly** (diamond open access, no fees; published under CC BY-ND). No fixed length limit ("submissions may be of any length", very long ones must merit the space). Note the No Derivatives licence differs from the CC BY 4.0 of the Zenodo version.

Also possible, limits not checked: *Journal of Roman Archaeology* (Cambridge; article word limits not stated on the author page; open access through agreements or waivers).

## Summary of the contribution (150 words)

Three classic claims about Latin inscriptions were made from hand-built samples: MacMullen's Severan peak of the epigraphic habit, Duncan-Jones's reading of age rounding on tombstones, and the growing localism of military recruitment. Using LIRE, a harmonised dataset of 203,466 Latin inscriptions from EDCS and EDH, I test each with methods fixed in a written plan beforehand: aoristic and Monte Carlo counts for uncertain dates, explicit and validated rules for reading ages and soldiers' origins, and clustered models. The post-Severan collapse is very large (more than twice as many inscriptions a year in 193 to 235 as in 250 to 300), but the empire-wide peak is in the first century; the Severan peak belongs to the provinces and to public and religious texts. Ages are rounded everywhere and more at older ages, but the African excess disappears once setting and age are held fixed. Soldiers' stated origins do not move closer to home, a sign of selection.
