Romans wrote on stone everywhere: on tombs, altars, statue bases, milestones and the walls of public buildings. The LIRE dataset gathers {{n.all|int}} Latin inscriptions from the two great online corpora, each with a findspot and a date range. Put them on one map and through one clock and three old questions come back into focus: when did the Romans write most, how old were the dead, and how far did soldiers travel?

01 · The mapItaly, the frontiers and the cities

Rome alone has {{n.rome|int}} inscriptions, {{n.rome_pc|pc}} of the whole corpus, and with the rest of Italy it holds {{n.rome_italy_pc|pc}}. Outside Italy the writing follows the army and the cities: the Rhine and Danube frontiers, the coast of Dalmatia around Salona, the towns of Africa around Carthage and the legionary fortress of Lambaesis, and southern Spain. Across the provinces of about AD 117 there are about {{density.empire_per1000|f0}} inscriptions per 1,000 km², excluding the city of Rome; Britain has {{density.britannia.per1000km2|f0}}, the Italian region around Rome and Naples {{density.top.0.per1000km2|int}}.

02 · WhenThe habit, and its end

In 1982 Ramsay MacMullen noticed that Latin inscriptions rise through the first and second centuries, peak under the Severan emperors and then fall sharply. With {{habit.main.n|int}} inscriptions whose date range is 100 years or less, the fall is plain: the empire produced {{habit.main.ratio|f1}} times as many inscriptions a year in 193 to 235 as in 250 to 300 (Monte Carlo interval {{habit.main.mc_ratio_lo|f2}} to {{habit.main.mc_ratio_hi|f2}}). But the empire-wide peak is not Severan. It falls in AD {{habit.main.peak_year|raw}} (95% interval AD {{habit.main.mc_peak_lo|raw}} to {{habit.main.mc_peak_hi|raw}}), because Rome and Italy, which dominate the corpus, wrote most in the first century. Region by region the picture splits: {{n.prov_peaks_severan|int}} of the {{n.prov_peaks|int}} provinces with at least 1,000 dated inscriptions peak between AD 175 and 250, among them Africa, Numidia, Dacia, both Pannonias and Lower Germany, while the Italian regions peak between the late first century BC and the later first century AD. Votive inscriptions peak in AD {{types.votive.peak_year|raw}}, honorific ones in {{types.honorific.peak_year|raw}}, milestones in {{types.milestone.peak_year|raw}}. 03 · AgesThe dead were rounded to fives

Many epitaphs give the age of the dead: vixit annis XXX, "lived 30 years". I read {{ages.n|int}} ages from {{ages.n_stones|int}} epitaphs. Thirty, forty and fifty are far too common: among ages from 23 to 62, {{ages.digits_23_62.0|int}} end in 0 and {{ages.digits_23_62.5|int}} in 5, against about {{ages.digit_expected|int}} expected for each digit without rounding. The Whipple index is {{ages.whipple.all.W|f0}} (95% CI {{ages.whipple.all.lo|f0}} to {{ages.whipple.all.hi|f0}}). Rounding is lightest in Rome ({{ages.whipple.Rome.W|f0}}) and heaviest in the Danube and Balkan provinces ({{ages.whipple.Danube and Balkans.W|f0}}); it is heavier in the countryside ({{ages.whipple.urban_rural.W|f0}}) than in large cities ({{ages.whipple.urban_large.W|f0}}), and it grows with age.

Richard Duncan-Jones read age rounding as a sign of how far people knew their exact age, and expected it to be heaviest in Africa. Africa's raw index is indeed higher than Rome's ({{ages.whipple.Africa.W|f0}} against {{ages.whipple.Rome.W|f0}}), but once the setting, the age and Christian epitaphs are taken into account the difference is not significant (odds ratio {{ages.model.africa_vs_rome.or|f2}}, 95% CI {{ages.model.africa_vs_rome.lo|f2}} to {{ages.model.africa_vs_rome.hi|f2}}). The countryside effect holds in the main model (odds ratio {{ages.model.rural_vs_large.or|f2}}, {{ages.model.rural_vs_large.lo|f2}} to {{ages.model.rural_vs_large.hi|f2}}) but not in every check.

Can tombstones tell us how long Romans lived? No, as Keith Hopkins argued in 1966. The mean age on the stones is {{ages.hopkins.by_group.Rome.mean|f0}} in Rome and {{ages.hopkins.by_group.Africa.mean|f0}} in Africa, a gap no real difference in mortality could make; only {{ages.hopkins.by_group.Africa.under10_pc|f0}}% of African ages are under 10, against {{ages.hopkins.by_group.Rome.under10_pc|f0}}% in Rome, when in any pre-modern population a large share of deaths were of young children; and {{ages.hopkins.by_group.Africa.n_100plus|int}} ages on African stones are 100 or more. The stones record who was commemorated, and how, not who died.

04 · SoldiersA long way from home

Soldiers' tombstones often say where the man came from: domo Verona, a voting tribe and a town (Pollia Mutina), or a people (natione Bessus, a Thracian). Of {{soldiers.n_stones|int}} military inscriptions, {{soldiers.n_mapped|int}} ({{soldiers.mapped_pc|f1}}%) state an origin I could place on the map. The median journey is {{soldiers.median_km|int}} km, and {{soldiers.pc_over_500|pc}} are more than 500 km: the fleet at Misenum and the guard in Rome drew men from Egypt, Thrace, Syria and Pannonia. Switch on "Soldiers' journeys" in the layers.

J. C. Mann and Giovanni Forni showed that recruitment became more local over time. These stones do not show it: those dated after AD 100 have a longer median journey ({{soldiers.h3.med_late|int}} km) than earlier ones ({{soldiers.h3.med_early|int}} km), and among origins given as towns there is no change at all (ratio {{soldiers.city_only.ratio|f2}}, 95% CI {{soldiers.city_only.lo|f2}} to {{soldiers.city_only.hi|f2}}). The likely reason is selection: a man who served near home had little reason to carve his origin, so the stones that state one are a sample of the far-travelled.

05 · How I did thisCounting with uncertain dates

Data. LIRE v2.1 merges the Epigraphik-Datenbank Clauss/Slaby and the Epigraphic Database Heidelberg and keeps Latin inscriptions with a findspot and a date range overlapping 50 BC to AD 350. Its types (epitaph, votive, honorific...) come from the EDH where it exists and otherwise from the LIRE authors' machine-learning classifier. Time. Each inscription's weight of one is spread evenly over the years of its date range (aoristic analysis); a Monte Carlo version draws one year per inscription, 1,000 times, for intervals. Ages. An age is "annis" or "annorum" followed by an intact Roman numeral, after "vixit" or in the form "annorum N", on epitaphs only, never years of service or marriage; 199 of 200 checked by hand were right. Soldiers. Origins read from "domo", "natione", "civis" or a voting tribe followed by a town, matched to the Digital Atlas of the Roman Empire; every origin used twice or more was checked by eye. The analysis plan, with the tests and what would count against each idea, was pre-specified in a written plan before any result was computed.

Checks: the age-rounding contrasts and the soldiers' distances under the planned alternatives.

What this cannot say. The inscriptions are the ones that survived, were found and were published, so Rome, Italy and well-excavated provinces are over-represented. Dates are editors' ranges, some conventional, and Christian epitaphs are dated late because they are Christian. Ages are what commemorators wrote. Soldiers' origins come from the minority of stones that name one.
